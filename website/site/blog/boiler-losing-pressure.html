Why does my boiler keep losing pressure?

By Wes, Gas Safe Plumber & Heating Engineer 30 October 2026 6 min read

The short answer: If your boiler pressure keeps dropping, something is letting water out. The three most common causes are a weeping pressure relief valve, a tired expansion vessel and a small leak in the system or at the boiler's own pipe connections. Topping up once after bleeding a radiator is normal. Doing it every few days means there is a real fault. My Southampton repairs for this symptom run from £100 to £315.

Pressure loss is the boiler fault I get called about most. The good news is that it is almost never a reason to replace the boiler. The bad news is that topping it up every week is not a fix: it means water is going somewhere, and that somewhere needs finding.

What is the correct boiler pressure?

Most combi and system boilers should read between 1 and 1.5 bar when cold. You can see the reading on the pressure gauge on the front of the boiler. At operating temperature the pressure rises to around 2 bar, which is normal.

Below 0.5 bar the boiler will usually stop and show a low pressure warning or error code. Above 3 bar the pressure relief valve opens to let water out. Both are the boiler protecting itself.

Is it safe to top up boiler pressure yourself?

Yes, for most boilers. The filling link (a braided hose under the boiler with a valve at each end) lets you add water from the mains. Open both valves slowly, watch the gauge rise to about 1.2 to 1.5 bar, then close both valves. If you cannot find the filling link or the pressure will not rise, stop and call an engineer. Do not force valves that feel stiff.

Topping up once after a radiator bleed or an engineer visit is fine. Topping up every few days tells you there is a fault worth finding.

The three most common causes

1. Pressure relief valve weeping

The pressure relief valve (PRV) is a safety device that opens if the system pressure gets too high. Over time the valve seat wears and it starts to let a small amount of water through even at normal pressure. The giveaway is a thin copper pipe on the outside of the house (usually near the boiler flue or on an outside wall) that drips or weeps water.

A new PRV is a straightforward repair. On my own 2026 jobs in Southampton it came to £120 to £150 all in.

2. Expansion vessel failure

Every sealed heating system has an expansion vessel: a pressurised container that absorbs the extra volume of water when it heats up. If the bladder inside fails or the pre-charge pressure drops too low, the vessel cannot do its job and the system pressure swings too high when it heats up. This triggers the PRV to release water, which is why the pressure then reads low once the boiler cools.

Sometimes the vessel only needs re-pressurising with a foot pump, which can fit within the first £100 hour. If the bladder has failed, a replacement vessel is needed. A new expansion vessel on a Vaillant came to £315 on my jobs this year.

3. A leak in the system or boiler pipework

Small leaks at joints, radiator valves or the boiler's own pipe connections can lose enough water over days to drop the pressure gauge without leaving an obvious puddle. Worth checking for damp patches under radiators and around the boiler before calling an engineer.

On my 2026 jobs, leaking internal boiler connections came to £149 to £196 depending on the part: supply manifold leaks on Worcester boilers came to £149 to £157, and a leaking filling loop on a Glow-worm was £196.

What about the filling loop itself?

A filling loop (or filling link) that does not close properly lets mains water slowly feed into the system, which pushes pressure up rather than causing a drop. That is a different symptom: pressure that climbs on its own rather than falling. The fix is the same category, though: a worn internal valve on the filling assembly.

What it almost never is

A boiler that only needs topping up is not evidence that the boiler needs replacing. If the first answer from a tradesperson is "you need a new boiler" before any diagnosis, ask for a specific part name. Pressure faults on a well-serviced boiler are almost always a single cheap component.

FAQS

People also ask

Why does my boiler keep losing pressure?

The three most common causes are a weeping pressure relief valve, a tired expansion vessel (either the bladder has failed or the pre-charge pressure is low), and a small leak somewhere in the system pipework or at the boiler's own water connections. Topping up the pressure once after bleeding a radiator or after an engineer visits is normal. Doing it every few days means something is genuinely losing water.

What is the correct boiler pressure?

For most combi and system boilers, the correct cold pressure is between 1 and 1.5 bar, shown on the pressure gauge on the boiler front panel. At full operating temperature it will rise to around 2 bar, which is normal. Below 0.5 bar the boiler will usually lock out. Above 3 bar the pressure relief valve will open to release water.

Can I top up boiler pressure myself?

Yes, if your boiler has an external filling loop (a braided hose under the boiler with two valves). Turn both valves slowly until the gauge reads around 1.2 to 1.5 bar, then close them again. If you cannot find the filling loop or the pressure will not rise, do not force it. Some boilers have an internal key-fill system, and a few models need an engineer to re-pressurise them.

What does it cost to fix a boiler losing pressure in Southampton?

On my own 2026 jobs: a new pressure relief valve came to between £120 and £150 all in. A new expansion vessel on a Vaillant was £315. A leaking supply manifold on a Worcester came to £149 to £157. A new filling loop on a Glow-worm was £196. The cheapest outcome is the expansion vessel only needing a re-pressurise, which usually fits in the first £100 hour.

Is low boiler pressure dangerous?

Low pressure itself is not dangerous. The boiler will lock out before it can run in an unsafe state. What you should not do is keep resetting and restarting a boiler that is locking out repeatedly without finding the cause, as repeated restarts on a fault can turn a cheap repair into a more expensive one.
